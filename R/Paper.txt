\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Applied researchers routinely employ Difference-in-Differences (DiD), staggered-adoption (CSDiD), and triple-difference (DDD) designs, yet the mechanics of how each method selects comparison units, constructs counterfactuals, and encodes identifying assumptions often remain opaque even to their practitioners. This paper introduces the Causal Assignment Tree (CAT), a rooted labeled tree that makes the counterfactual structure of DiD estimators visually explicit and formally precise. We provide a formal definition of the CAT, establish nonparametric identification of the ATT under a cohort-specific conditional parallel trends assumption stated directly in terms of the tree's leaf pairs (Proposition 1), prove that the CAT taxonomy is complete in the sense that every valid DiD, CSDiD, and DDD estimand corresponds to a unique root-to-leaf-pair path (Proposition 2), and show that a sufficient condition for two-way fixed effects consistency is that the CAT contains no dual-role leaves, and characterize when their presence generically implies inconsistency (Corollary 1). Monte Carlo evidence confirms that TWFE exhibits bias of approximately 12 percent of the true effect under heterogeneous treatment timing when early adopters carry the highest treatment effects, while CSDiD-CAT and DDD-CAT estimators are approximately unbiased with coverage close to the nominal 95 percent level. Two empirical applications covering psychedelics deprioritization across California cities (CSDiD) and Medicaid expansion across United States hospitals (DDD) demonstrate the CAT's practical value for communicating identification strategies. The framework is implemented in the open-source catviz R package, available on CRAN.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent Keywords: Difference-in-Differences, Staggered Adoption, Counterfactual Reasoning, Causal Inference, Visualization, CSDiD, Triple Differences, Policy Evaluation.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent JEL Codes: C14, C15, C21, C23, I13, I18, K42.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Every causal question boils down to a comparison between two states of the world: what did happen, and what would have happened under an alternative scenario. This unobserved alternative, the counterfactual, is the foundation of causal inference. No estimator, no matter how sophisticated, can escape this fundamental fact: causal effects are defined as the difference between observed outcomes and counterfactual outcomes we can never directly observe.

In randomized controlled trials, this problem has an elegant solution. Random assignment ensures that untreated units provide a valid stand-in for what would have happened to treated units had they not received treatment. The counterfactual is, by design, directly observable in the control group.

But in observational settings, where researchers study naturally occurring policy changes, business decisions, or economic shocks, we do not have the luxury of randomization. Instead, we must construct the counterfactual from available data, relying on assumptions, identification strategies, and statistical models. This is where Difference-in-Differences (DiD) methods come in.

DiD is one of the workhorses of modern applied economics and policy research \citep{Angrist2009, Imbens2015}. The basic intuition is simple: compare how outcomes evolved over time for treated units versus control units. If both groups would have trended similarly absent treatment, the difference in their trends identifies the causal effect.

This intuition extends naturally to more complex settings. When units adopt treatment at different times, the Callaway and Sant'Anna (CSDiD) estimator \citep{Callaway2021} allows each cohort to have its own treatment effect, using not-yet-treated or never-treated units as comparisons. When treatment effects vary across observable subgroups, the Difference-in-Difference-in-Differences (DDD) design \citep{Gruber1994}, extended to staggered adoption by \citet{OrtizVillavicencio2025}, uses within-cohort comparisons to net out common time-varying shocks.

Despite their widespread use, these methods can feel like a black box to applied researchers and policymakers. The algebraic expressions are dense. The estimation procedures involving doubly robust weighting, GMM aggregation, and multiplier bootstrap are procedurally complex. And crucially, it is often unclear which units are actually being used as controls, and how.

This opacity matters. Understanding who serves as the counterfactual is essential for evaluating whether an identification strategy is credible. Are the comparison units truly similar to the treated units? Are we comparing apples to apples? Which time periods matter? What assumptions are we really making?

\subsection{The Contribution}

This paper introduces the Causal Assignment Tree (CAT), a tree-based visualization that makes the counterfactual structure of DiD estimators explicit and formally grounded.

The CAT shows at a glance: (1) treatment assignment, specifically how the tree partitions all units by treatment timing (cohorts) and subgroups; (2) comparison groups, showing exactly which units serve as controls for each treated cohort; (3) counterfactual construction, revealing how the counterfactual outcome is built from comparison units; and (4) sample sizes, displaying how many units fall into each group.

Beyond visualization, the CAT provides a formal framework for stating and verifying identifying assumptions. Assumptions that are typically written as abstract population equalities can be restated as conditions on specific pairs of leaves in the tree, making their content transparent and their plausibility assessable.

\subsection{CAT Compared to Existing Visualization Tools}
\label{subsec:compare}

The CAT occupies a distinct niche relative to existing tools for communicating causal research designs.

Directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) \citep{Pearl2009} encode structural assumptions about why variables are causally related, capturing the underlying data-generating process. The CAT, by contrast, encodes estimation mechanics: which units serve as controls, in which periods, and under which identifying assumptions. The two frameworks are complementary. A researcher should first draw a DAG to establish the causal question, then draw a CAT to communicate how the estimator answers it.

Event study figures display estimated dynamic treatment effects over calendar time. They show results. The CAT shows design, specifically which comparison groups make those results possible. The CAT should be drawn before running event study regressions; it makes explicit which comparison units generate the estimated dynamic effects.

Goodman-Bacon's decomposition \citep{GoodmanBacon2021} is a powerful diagnostic for understanding the implicit 2$\times$2 comparisons embedded in a TWFE regression. The CAT generalizes this concept to DDD designs and makes subgroup splits explicit in the tree topology. Moreover, the CAT is constructive, guiding the researcher toward valid estimators, whereas the checkerboard decomposition is primarily diagnostic.

Covariate balance tables assess whether treated and comparison units are similar on observed covariates. The CAT identifies which comparison units should appear in that balance table. Both tools are needed: the CAT first, the balance table second.

\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:lit}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

This paper contributes to two broad literatures: modern Difference-in-Differences methods and causal inference visualization.

\subsection{Difference-in-Differences and Staggered Adoption}

The canonical 2$\times$2 DiD design has been a staple of empirical economics since at least \citet{Card1994}. The key identifying assumption, parallel trends, requires that treated and control units would have evolved similarly absent treatment.

Recent work has highlighted important challenges when treatment is staggered or when treatment effects are heterogeneous. \citet{GoodmanBacon2021} shows that two-way fixed effects regressions can produce misleading estimates when treatment timing varies, because they implicitly use already-treated units as controls for later-treated units. \citet{Callaway2021} develop the CSDiD estimator, which avoids this problem by estimating cohort-specific treatment effects and using only not-yet-treated or never-treated units as comparisons.

Other important contributions include \citet{SunAbraham2021}, who propose interaction-weighted estimators; \citet{BorusyakJaravel2021}, who develop imputation-based approaches; and \citet{DeChaisemartin2020}, who characterize when TWFE estimates are valid. \citet{Roth2023} provide practical guidance on pre-trends testing and sensitivity analysis. \citet{SantAnnaZhao2020} develop doubly robust DiD estimators that combine outcome regression and inverse probability weighting, providing semiparametric efficiency and robustness to model misspecification. \citet{RambachanRoth2023} propose a framework for sensitivity analysis that relaxes the parallel trends assumption in a controlled way, allowing researchers to assess how much violations of parallel trends would change their conclusions. \citet{Baker2022} document pervasive problems with staggered DiD in corporate finance applications, showing that TWFE estimates can be severely biased in realistic settings. \citet{Wooldridge2021} provides a unifying perspective showing that the two-way Mundlak regression encompasses many modern DiD estimators as special cases. \citet{Arkhangelsky2021} develop synthetic difference-in-differences, combining the synthetic control method with DiD to improve pre-period fit. \citet{DeChaisemartin2022} survey the rapidly growing literature on heterogeneous treatment effects in TWFE designs.

The triple-difference design extends DiD by adding a second differencing dimension, typically across subgroups \citep{Gruber1994, OrtizVillavicencio2025}. By comparing how treatment-control differences evolve differently across subgroups, DDD can relax some parallel trends assumptions or identify more granular treatment effects.

\subsection{Causal Diagrams and Visualization}

Causal diagrams have a long history in statistics and epidemiology, particularly directed acyclic graphs as formalized by \citet{Pearl2009}. In economics, causal diagrams have been less widely adopted, though see \citet{Imbens2020} for a discussion and \citet{HuntingtonKlein2021} for practical guidance.

This paper's CAT framework differs from DAGs in the important ways discussed in Section \ref{subsec:compare}. To our knowledge, this is the first paper to formalize DiD identification in terms of a rooted labeled tree, connecting the visualization directly to identifying assumptions via a formal assignment function.

\section{The Causal Assignment Tree: Formal Framework}
\label{sec:formal}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

This section formally defines the CAT, states the identifying assumptions in terms of the tree structure, and establishes identification, completeness, and a TWFE validity condition.

\subsection{Setup and Notation}

We observe a panel of $N$ units indexed by $i = 1, \ldots, N$ over $T$ periods indexed by $t = 1, \ldots, T$. Each unit is characterized by:
\begin{itemize}
\item A first treatment period $G_i \in \{g_1, g_2, \ldots, g_K, \infty\}$, where $G_i = \infty$ denotes a never-treated unit.
\item An optional binary subgroup indicator $Q_i \in \{0, 1\}$, used in DDD designs.
\item A vector of pre-treatment covariates $X_i$.
\item Potential outcomes $Y_{it}(0)$ (untreated) and $Y_{it}(1)$ (treated), with observed outcome $Y_{it} = Y_{it}(0) + (Y_{it}(1) - Y_{it}(0)) \cdot \mathbf{1}\{G_i \leq t\}$.
\end{itemize}

The object of interest is the group-time average treatment effect on the treated, defined for each fixed calendar period $t \geq g$:
\[
\text{ATT}(g, t) \equiv \mathbb{E}[Y_{it}(1) - Y_{it}(0) \mid G_i = g], \quad t \geq g,
\]
where $t$ indexes a fixed calendar period, not a random variable.

\subsection{Definition of the Causal Assignment Tree}

\begin{definition}[Causal Assignment Tree]
A Causal Assignment Tree is a rooted labeled tree $\mathcal{T} = (V, E, \varphi)$ where:
\begin{itemize}
\item $V$ is a finite set of nodes with a designated root $r \in V$.
\item $E \subseteq V \times V$ is a set of directed edges from parent to child.
\item $\varphi: \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{T}) \to \mathcal{G} \times \mathcal{P} \times \mathcal{Q}$ is an assignment function that maps each leaf node $\ell \in \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{T})$ to a unique cell in the product space of cohorts, time periods ($\mathcal{P} = \{1,\ldots,T\}$), and subgroups.
\end{itemize}
The root node contains all sample units. Each internal node is split along one of three dimensions: treatment cohort ($G_i = g$), pre/post timing ($t < g$ vs.\ $t \geq g$), or subgroup ($Q_i = 0$ vs.\ $Q_i = 1$). Each leaf node thus represents a mutually exclusive and exhaustive partition of the sample.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
The assignment function $\varphi$ makes explicit which units are in each cell. This is the tree's key feature: it renders the comparison group selection visible and verifiable.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Identifying Assumptions in CAT Notation}

\begin{assumption}[Stable Unit Treatment Value]
\label{ass:sutva}
Potential outcomes are well-defined: $Y_{it}(d)$ depends only on unit $i$'s own treatment status $d \in \{0,1\}$ and not on the treatment status of other units.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[No Anticipation]
\label{ass:na}
For all $t < G_i$: $Y_{it}(d) = Y_{it}(0)$ for all $d$. Pre-treatment outcomes are not affected by future treatment.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Random Sampling]
\label{ass:rs}
$\{Y_{it}(0), Y_{it}(1), G_i, Q_i, X_i\}_{i=1}^N$ are i.i.d.\ draws from the population distribution.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Strong Overlap]
\label{ass:overlap}
For each cohort $g$ and subgroup $q$: $0 < \Pr(G_i = g \mid X_i) < 1$ and $0 < \Pr(Q_i = q \mid X_i) < 1$ almost surely.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[CAT Conditional Parallel Trends]
\label{ass:pt}
For each pair of leaf nodes $(\ell_T, \ell_C)$ in $\mathcal{T}$ representing a treated leaf and its designated comparison leaf, respectively:
\[
\mathbb{E}[Y_{it}(0) - Y_{i,g-1}(0) \mid i \in \ell_T, X_i] = \mathbb{E}[Y_{it}(0) - Y_{i,g-1}(0) \mid i \in \ell_C, X_i], \quad t \geq g.
\]
In words: absent treatment, units in the treated leaf would have trended the same as units in the comparison leaf, conditional on covariates.
\end{assumption}

\begin{remark}
Assumption \ref{ass:pt} is a cohort-specific conditional parallel trends assumption. It is weaker than the unconditional parallel trends assumed in the canonical 2$\times$2 DiD, which requires the equality to hold marginally rather than conditionally. Unconditional parallel trends follows as a special case when $X_i$ is empty.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Main Results}

The following result applies the identification argument of \citet{Callaway2021} within the CAT framework. The novel content is that the leaf-pair notation makes explicit \emph{which specific units} must satisfy the conditional parallel trends condition, rendering the assumption operationally verifiable from the tree diagram.

\begin{proposition}[CAT Identification; cf.\ \citealt{Callaway2021}, Proposition~1]
\label{prop:id}
Under Assumptions \ref{ass:sutva}--\ref{ass:pt} with $X_i$ empty (unconditional parallel trends), for each treated leaf $\ell_T$ and its comparison leaf $\ell_C$ in $\mathcal{T}$, the group-time ATT is nonparametrically identified:
\[
\text{ATT}(g, t) = \mathbb{E}[Y_{it} \mid i \in \ell_T] - \mathbb{E}[Y_{i,g-1} \mid i \in \ell_T] - \left(\mathbb{E}[Y_{it} \mid i \in \ell_C] - \mathbb{E}[Y_{i,g-1} \mid i \in \ell_C]\right).
\]
Under conditional parallel trends with non-empty $X_i$, identification obtains via the doubly robust estimator of \citet{SantAnnaZhao2020}, replacing leaf means with IPW- or outcome-regression-adjusted means.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
By Assumption~\ref{ass:na}, pre-treatment outcomes equal untreated potential outcomes: $\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,g-1} \mid i \in \ell_T] = \mathbb{E}[Y_{i,g-1}(0) \mid i \in \ell_T]$. Decompose the counterfactual mean:
\[
\mathbb{E}[Y_{it}(0) \mid i \in \ell_T] = \mathbb{E}[Y_{i,g-1}(0) \mid i \in \ell_T] + \mathbb{E}[Y_{it}(0) - Y_{i,g-1}(0) \mid i \in \ell_T].
\]
By Assumption~\ref{ass:pt} (unconditional), $\mathbb{E}[Y_{it}(0) - Y_{i,g-1}(0) \mid i \in \ell_T] = \mathbb{E}[Y_{it}(0) - Y_{i,g-1}(0) \mid i \in \ell_C]$. Since $\ell_C$ contains only untreated units at time $t$ (by construction of the CAT), Assumption~\ref{ass:na} gives $\mathbb{E}[Y_{it}(0) - Y_{i,g-1}(0) \mid i \in \ell_C] = \mathbb{E}[Y_{it} - Y_{i,g-1} \mid i \in \ell_C]$. Substituting into $\text{ATT}(g,t) = \mathbb{E}[Y_{it} \mid i \in \ell_T] - \mathbb{E}[Y_{it}(0) \mid i \in \ell_T]$ yields the stated formula.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[CAT Completeness]
\label{prop:complete}
Every valid DiD, CSDiD, and DDD estimand corresponds to a unique root-to-leaf-pair path in the appropriate CAT. Conversely, every root-to-leaf-pair path in the CAT corresponds to a well-defined estimand.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
\textit{Forward direction.} A DiD estimand is indexed by a (treated cohort $g$, comparison group $c$, period $t$) triple. By Definition~1, each leaf $\ell \in \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{T})$ is the unique cell containing all units with a specific $(G_i, Q_i)$ assignment; a root-to-leaf path traces the splitting decisions that define this cell. A leaf pair $(\ell_T, \ell_C)$ encodes a unique $(g, c)$ pair: $\ell_T$ specifies the treated cohort and subgroup, $\ell_C$ specifies the comparison group and subgroup. In the CSDiD case the relevant object is a 2-leaf pair; in the DDD case it is a 4-leaf tuple $(\ell_{T,Q=1}, \ell_{T,Q=0}, \ell_{C,Q=1}, \ell_{C,Q=0})$. Since the partition is exhaustive and mutually exclusive (Definition~1), each valid estimand maps to exactly one such path.

\textit{Converse direction.} Every root-to-leaf-pair path $(\ell_T, \ell_C)$ defines a candidate estimand. The estimand is well-defined and identified if: (i) $\ell_T$ contains pre-treatment observations (baseline is observed), (ii) $\ell_C$ contains no already-treated units at time $t$ (the no-dual-role condition of Corollary~1), (iii) the leaves are non-empty (Assumption~\ref{ass:overlap}), and (iv) Assumption~\ref{ass:pt} holds for the pair. Under these conditions, Proposition~1 guarantees identification.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[TWFE Validity]
\label{cor:twfe}
Two-way fixed effects regression produces non-negative implicit weights on all cohort-time ATTs---and hence is consistent for a valid positively weighted average treatment effect---if and only if the CAT contains no dual-role leaves, where a leaf has a \emph{dual role} if it serves as a treated leaf in one comparison and as a comparison leaf in another. Under non-degenerate cohort-heterogeneous treatment effects, the presence of any dual-role leaf implies TWFE inconsistency for the population-weighted average ATT.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
\citet{GoodmanBacon2021} establishes that the TWFE estimator equals a weighted average of all implicit 2$\times$2 DiD comparisons, where weights depend on cell sizes and timing and can be negative. A negative-weight comparison arises precisely when a leaf $\ell$ that was previously treated is placed in the comparison group for a later-treated cohort---the dual-role condition. When $\ell$ carries a dual role and its cohort's treatment effect is non-zero, the comparison group mean reflects its own treatment, contaminating the implicit DiD and producing a negative implicit weight. Conversely, if no leaf has a dual role, every implicit 2$\times$2 comparison uses clean, untreated comparison units, all Goodman-Bacon weights are non-negative, and TWFE is consistent for a positively weighted average of cohort-time ATTs. The necessity under non-degenerate heterogeneity follows because the contaminating weight equals a strictly positive function of cell sizes times the already-treated cohort's treatment effect, which is non-zero whenever that effect is non-zero.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The CAT as a Research Design Checklist}

The propositions above translate directly into an applied research protocol: draw the CAT before estimation, verify that every treated leaf has a clean comparison leaf (Propositions~1--2), confirm that no leaf carries a dual role (Corollary~1), and check that each leaf contains enough units (Assumption~\ref{ass:overlap}). A concrete three-step implementation of this checklist---using the catviz package---is developed in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

\section{Difference-in-Differences Using the Causal Assignment Tree}
\label{sec:did}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

We begin with the simplest case: the canonical 2$\times$2 Difference-in-Differences design. This is the building block for understanding CSDiD and DDD.

\subsection{A Motivating Example: When TWFE Misleads}

Before introducing the CAT, consider a concrete example that illustrates why visualization matters. Suppose a researcher studies the effect of a minimum wage increase adopted by some states in 2015 and others in 2018. A standard TWFE regression with state and year fixed effects is easy to run and produces a precise estimate. But what comparison is actually being made?

Goodman-Bacon's decomposition reveals that some of the identifying variation comes from comparing states that adopted in 2018 to states that adopted in 2015, using the 2015 adopters as controls during their own post-treatment period. If the 2015 wage increase has lasting effects, this comparison is contaminated: the ``control'' group is not at its baseline level.

Without a visualization, this contamination is invisible. The TWFE output looks clean. The CAT makes the contamination visible instantly: if any leaf in the tree contains already-treated units serving as controls, the researcher sees it immediately.

\subsection{The Fundamental Problem: Missing Counterfactuals}

Every causal question asks for a comparison between two states:
\begin{itemize}
\item What did happen: the observed outcome for treated units after treatment, $Y^{\text{post}}_{\text{treated}}$.
\item What would have happened: the counterfactual outcome for those same units if they had not been treated, $Y^{\text{post}}_{\text{counterfactual}}$.
\end{itemize}

The average treatment effect on the treated is simply:
\[
\text{ATT} = Y^{\text{post}}_{\text{treated}} - Y^{\text{post}}_{\text{counterfactual}}.
\]

The problem? We never observe $Y^{\text{post}}_{\text{counterfactual}}$. By definition, treated units were treated, so we cannot directly see what would have happened to them without treatment. This is the fundamental problem of causal inference \citep{Holland1986}.

DiD solves this problem by using a control group to construct the counterfactual.

\subsection{The Canonical 2$\times$2 DiD Formula}

The canonical DiD estimator compares the before-after change for treated units to the before-after change for control units:
\[
\text{ATT} = \left( \bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{\text{treated}} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{\text{treated}} \right) - \left( \bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{\text{control}} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{\text{control}} \right).
\]

This is the four averages and three subtractions formula. It requires four group means: treated units before and after treatment, and control units before and after the treatment period.

\subsection{The CAT for 2$\times$2 DiD}

Figure \ref{fig:did_cat} shows the Causal Assignment Tree for this design.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{did.png}
    \caption{Causal Assignment Tree for 2$\times$2 Difference-in-Differences. All 100 units are split into treated (A) and control (B) groups, then further split by pre/post periods. Leaf nodes C, D, E, F represent the four group-time cells used in the DiD formula.}
    \label{fig:did_cat}
\end{figure}

The tree has a simple structure. The root node contains all units in the sample. Internal node $A$ labels the treated branch and $B$ the control branch; these appear in the figure for orientation but do not enter the DiD formula directly. The second split separates pre-treatment from post-treatment periods, yielding four leaf nodes: $C$ (treated, pre-treatment), $D$ (treated, post-treatment), $E$ (control, pre-treatment), and $F$ (control, post-treatment). Labels A through F in this section are specific to this 2$\times$2 design.

Using this notation, the DiD formula becomes:
\[
\widehat{\text{ATT}}_{\text{DiD}} = (\bar{Y}_D - \bar{Y}_C) - (\bar{Y}_F - \bar{Y}_E).
\]

This is the same formula, written using the CAT labels. The tree makes it visually clear which groups are being compared.

\subsection{Selection Bias and the Parallel Trends Assumption}

In observational data, treated and control units are usually different before treatment. The naive post-treatment comparison $\bar{Y}_D - \bar{Y}_F$ mixes the treatment effect with pre-existing differences:
\[
\mathbb{E}[Y_D] - \mathbb{E}[Y_F] = \underbrace{\text{ATT}}_{\text{treatment effect}} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Y_D(0)] - \mathbb{E}[Y_F]}_{\text{selection bias}}.
\]

DiD solves this by assuming that, even if treated and control units start at different levels, they would have evolved similarly absent treatment. Formally (Assumption \ref{ass:pt} in the 2$\times$2 case):
\[
\mathbb{E}[Y_D(0)] - \mathbb{E}[Y_C(0)] = \mathbb{E}[Y_F] - \mathbb{E}[Y_E].
\]

This assumption allows us to construct the counterfactual as:
\[
\mathbb{E}[Y_D(0)] = \mathbb{E}[Y_C] + \left( \mathbb{E}[Y_F] - \mathbb{E}[Y_E] \right),
\]
where the second equality uses Assumption \ref{ass:na}: pre-treatment outcomes for treated units equal their untreated potential outcomes.

Substituting into the ATT definition:
\[
\widehat{\text{ATT}}_{\text{DiD}} = (\bar{Y}_D - \bar{Y}_C) - (\bar{Y}_F - \bar{Y}_E).
\]

\begin{remark}[Parallel Trends in the CAT]
The parallel trends assumption says: the time trend from leaf $C$ to leaf $D$ that the treated units would have experienced without treatment equals the time trend from leaf $E$ to leaf $F$ that the control units actually experienced. By reading the tree, a researcher can immediately ask whether this comparison is credible, given who is in each leaf.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Inference via the Multiplier Bootstrap}

Standard errors for $\widehat{\text{ATT}}_{\text{DiD}}$ are obtained via the multiplier bootstrap \citep{Callaway2021}. The estimator can be written as a sum of unit-level influence functions:
\[
\widehat{\text{ATT}}_{\text{DiD}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \psi_i.
\]

For each bootstrap replication $b = 1, \ldots, B$, draw random multipliers $V_i^{(b)}$ with mean zero and variance one (e.g., Rademacher weights) and compute:
\[
\widehat{\text{ATT}}^{(b)}_{\text{DiD}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} V_i^{(b)} \psi_i.
\]

The standard error is the standard deviation of $\{\widehat{\text{ATT}}^{(b)}_{\text{DiD}}\}_{b=1}^B$, and a 95\% confidence interval uses the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. This bootstrap is valid in panel data because each unit $i$ receives one multiplier applied to all its time periods, preserving the within-unit correlation structure.

\section{CSDiD: Staggered Adoption with the Causal Assignment Tree}
\label{sec:csdid}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

In the previous section, we saw how the CAT clarifies identification in the simplest DiD setting. But real-world policies are rarely adopted all at once. More often, different units adopt treatment at different times, which is called staggered adoption.

The Callaway and Sant'Anna estimator \citep{Callaway2021} extends DiD to handle staggered adoption while allowing treatment effects to differ across cohorts and over time.

\subsection{Setting: Multiple Treatment Cohorts}

Units are indexed by $i$ and observed over periods $t = 1, \ldots, T$. Each unit belongs to a treatment cohort $G_i$, defined as its first treatment period, or $G_i = \infty$ if never treated.

Our goal is to estimate the group-time average treatment effect:
\[
\text{ATT}(g, t) \equiv \mathbb{E}[Y_{it}(1) - Y_{it}(0) \mid G_i = g], \quad t \geq g.
\]

\subsection{The CAT for Staggered Adoption}

Figure \ref{fig:csdid_tree} shows the Causal Assignment Tree for a staggered adoption design.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{csdid.png}
    \caption{Causal Assignment Tree for staggered treatment adoption (CSDiD). The tree shows 100 units split into treated cohorts (A through F) by first treatment year, plus a never-treated group (G). Each treated cohort uses not-yet-treated and never-treated units as comparisons.}
    \label{fig:csdid_tree}
\end{figure}

The root contains all units. The first split separates treated cohorts from the never-treated group (G). The treated branch is further split by first treatment time, yielding cohorts A through F.

\subsection{Identification}

For compactness, write $\mathbb{E}[Y_t(\ell)] \equiv \mathbb{E}[Y_{it} \mid i \in \ell,\, t]$ for any leaf $\ell \in \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{T})$ and calendar period $t$. Leaf labels A through G in this section refer specifically to the CSDiD design of Figure~\ref{fig:csdid_tree} and are not carried over from earlier sections.

For cohort $g$ at time $t \geq g$, valid comparison units are those not yet treated by time $t$, or never treated. CSDiD allows both types. In CAT notation, comparing cohort $A$ (first treated, label $g = 2015$) to the never-treated group $G$ gives:
\[
\text{ATT}(A, t) = \mathbb{E}[Y_t(A)] - \mathbb{E}[Y_t(G)].
\]

When pooling all not-yet-treated units, the comparison group expands:
\begin{align*}
\text{ATT}(A, t) &= \mathbb{E}[Y_t(A)] - \mathbb{E}[Y_t(B, C, D, E, F, G)], \\
\text{ATT}(B, t) &= \mathbb{E}[Y_t(B)] - \mathbb{E}[Y_t(C, D, E, F, G)], \\
&\vdots \\
\text{ATT}(F, t) &= \mathbb{E}[Y_t(F)] - \mathbb{E}[Y_t(G)].
\end{align*}

The CAT makes two things immediately visible. First, the comparison group shrinks for later cohorts. Second, cohort $F$ (the last adopter) can only use the never-treated group as a comparison, so its effect is identified solely from that comparison.

\begin{remark}[Parallel Trends for CSDiD]
For each cohort $g$, CSDiD requires:
\[
\mathbb{E}[Y_t(0) - Y_{g-1}(0) \mid G_i=g] = \mathbb{E}[Y_t(0) - Y_{g-1}(0) \mid i \in \ell_C^g],
\]
where $\ell_C^g$ is the comparison leaf designated in the CAT for cohort $g$ (the not-yet-treated or never-treated leaf). This is precisely Assumption~\ref{ass:pt} restricted to the leaf pair $(\ell_T, \ell_C^g)$: the pre-treatment time trends within these two leaves must be equal in expectation.
\end{remark}

When pooling not-yet-treated comparison units, CSDiD employs a doubly robust estimator combining outcome regression and inverse probability weighting. This estimator is consistent if either the outcome model or the propensity score model is correctly specified, providing two independent chances to recover the correct answer.

\subsection{Aggregation}

CSDiD estimates ATT$(g, t)$ for each cohort-time pair. To get an overall treatment effect, the group-time effects are aggregated:
\[
\Theta_{\text{overall}} = \sum_t \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}_t} w_{g,t} \, \text{ATT}(g, t),
\]
where $w_{g,t}$ are weights reflecting each cohort's sample size and post-treatment exposure.

\subsection{Inference}

Inference proceeds via the multiplier bootstrap as described in Section \ref{sec:did}. The procedure is identical, applied to each cohort-specific estimator, with the bootstrap drawing one multiplier per unit and applying it across all time periods and cohorts.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{DDD: Exploiting Subgroup Variation with the CAT}
\label{sec:ddd}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The Difference-in-Difference-in-Differences design adds a third layer of differencing: variation across subgroups within each treatment cohort \citep{Gruber1994, OrtizVillavicencio2025}.

The idea is: if treatment affects one subgroup but not another, we can use the untreated subgroup as an additional control to net out common shocks affecting both subgroups.

\subsection{Setting: Cohorts with Subgroups}

As before, units are indexed by first treatment time $G \in \{2015, \ldots, 2023, \infty\}$. Within each cohort $g$, units are partitioned into two subgroups:
\begin{itemize}
\item $Q = 1$: the subgroup expected to be affected by treatment.
\item $Q = 0$: the subgroup not expected to be affected.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The CAT for DDD}

Figure \ref{fig:ddd_tree} shows the Causal Assignment Tree for a DDD design.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{ddd.png}
    \caption{Causal Assignment Tree for DDD. Each treatment cohort (2015 through 2023) is split into treated subgroup ($Q=1$) and untreated subgroup ($Q=0$). The never-treated cohort is also split by subgroup. Labels A through N identify each cohort-subgroup combination.}
    \label{fig:ddd_tree}
\end{figure}

The root contains all units. The first split separates cohorts. Within each cohort, the second split separates subgroup $Q=1$ from $Q=0$. Each leaf node thus represents a unique (cohort, subgroup) combination, and the tree has two additional levels compared to the CSDiD tree. Leaf labels A through N in this section refer specifically to the seven-cohort DDD design of Figure~\ref{fig:ddd_tree} and are not carried over from earlier sections.

\subsection{Identification}

For cohort $g = 2015$ at time $t$, the DDD estimator using never-treated units as comparison is:
\begin{align*}
\text{ATT}_{2015,\infty} &= \left[ (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{A,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{A,t}) - (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{B,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{B,t}) \right] \\
&\quad - \left[ (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{M,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{M,t}) - (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{N,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{N,t}) \right].
\end{align*}

The first bracketed term measures how the $Q=1$ vs.\ $Q=0$ gap changed within the treated cohort. The second bracketed term measures the same gap change within the never-treated cohort. The difference, the third difference, is the extra change attributable to treatment.

Using not-yet-treated cohorts as additional comparisons yields five further estimates for the 2015 cohort, each differing only in the comparison leaves used:
\begin{align*}
\text{ATT}_{2015,2016} &= \left[ (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{A,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{A,t}) - (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{B,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{B,t}) \right]
- \left[ (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{C,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{C,t}) - (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{D,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{D,t}) \right], \\[0.5em]
\text{ATT}_{2015,2019} &= \left[ (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{A,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{A,t}) - (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{B,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{B,t}) \right]
- \left[ (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{E,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{E,t}) - (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{F,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{F,t}) \right], \\[0.5em]
\text{ATT}_{2015,2020} &= \left[ (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{A,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{A,t}) - (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{B,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{B,t}) \right]
- \left[ (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{G,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{G,t}) - (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{H,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{H,t}) \right], \\[0.5em]
\text{ATT}_{2015,2021} &= \left[ (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{A,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{A,t}) - (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{B,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{B,t}) \right]
- \left[ (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{I,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{I,t}) - (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{J,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{J,t}) \right], \\[0.5em]
\text{ATT}_{2015,2023} &= \left[ (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{A,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{A,t}) - (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{B,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{B,t}) \right]
- \left[ (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{K,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{K,t}) - (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{L,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{L,t}) \right].
\end{align*}

These six estimates---five not-yet-treated and one never-treated---are combined via GMM:
\[
\text{ATT}_{2015} = \text{GMM}\!\left(\text{ATT}_{2015,2016},\ \text{ATT}_{2015,2019},\ \text{ATT}_{2015,2020},\ \text{ATT}_{2015,2021},\ \text{ATT}_{2015,2023},\ \text{ATT}_{2015,\infty}\right).
\]

The complete analogous expressions for cohorts 2016 through 2023 are provided in the Appendix.

\begin{remark}[DDD Conditional Parallel Trends]
DDD requires a stronger parallel trends condition: absent treatment, the treated subgroup ($Q=1$) in cohort $g$ would have trended the same as the untreated subgroup ($Q=0$) in the comparison cohorts. In CAT notation, this is a condition on four leaves simultaneously: $(\ell_{T,Q=1}, \ell_{T,Q=0}, \ell_{C,Q=1}, \ell_{C,Q=0})$.

The CAT makes this assumption visible: a researcher can immediately see whether the four leaves are composed of plausibly comparable units.
\end{remark}

The four identifying assumptions for DDD (Assumptions \ref{ass:sutva} through \ref{ass:pt}) apply with the conditional parallel trends now stated for subgroup-cohort pairs. These are given formally in the general framework of Section \ref{sec:formal}.

\subsection{Aggregation via GMM}

DDD produces multiple estimates per cohort: one for each valid comparison cohort. This over-identification is resolved using GMM. For cohort $g$ with comparison estimates stacked in vector $\widehat{\mathbf{ATT}}_g$ and variance-covariance matrix $\widehat{\Omega}_g$, the efficient GMM estimator is:
\[
\widehat{\text{ATT}}^{\text{GMM}}_g = \frac{\mathbf{1}' \widehat{\Omega}_g^{-1} \widehat{\mathbf{ATT}}_g}{\mathbf{1}' \widehat{\Omega}_g^{-1} \mathbf{1}}.
\]

This is the efficient GMM estimator of \citet{OrtizVillavicencio2025}, adapted to CAT notation; it combines estimates from all valid comparison cohorts, giving more weight to more precise comparisons. The overall ATT is then:
\[
\widehat{\Theta}_{\text{DDD}} = \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}} w_g \, \widehat{\text{ATT}}^{\text{GMM}}_g, \quad w_g = n_g \Big/ \sum_{g'} n_{g'},
\]
following the cohort-size weighting convention of \citet{Callaway2021}.

\begin{remark}[GMM with Never-Treated and Not-Yet-Treated Comparisons]
\label{rem:gmm}
The GMM formula treats never-treated and not-yet-treated comparisons as moment conditions with the same structure, but they rest on distinct identifying assumptions and have different estimation windows. The never-treated comparison ($\text{ATT}_{g,\infty}$) is valid for all post-treatment periods $t \geq g$ and requires parallel trends against units permanently out of treatment. The not-yet-treated comparison ($\text{ATT}_{g,g'}$ for $g' > g$) is valid only for periods $t < g'$, the window before comparison cohort $g'$ receives treatment; once $g'$ adopts, those units are no longer clean controls. GMM accounts for these differences automatically: comparisons with shorter estimation windows have larger standard errors and receive lower weight through $\widehat{\Omega}_g^{-1}$. Researchers should nonetheless verify that each not-yet-treated comparison has at least two or three pre-treatment periods to permit pre-trend testing; when this condition fails, the affected moment should be excluded from the GMM rather than down-weighted.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Inference}

Inference uses the multiplier bootstrap as before, now applied to the over-identified DDD system. Each unit receives one multiplier applied across all its time periods and subgroup observations.

\section{Monte Carlo Evidence}
\label{sec:mc}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

This section presents Monte Carlo simulation evidence comparing TWFE, CSDiD-CAT, and DDD-CAT estimators under heterogeneous treatment timing and treatment effects.

\subsection{Data-Generating Process}

We simulate panel data with $N=500$ units observed over $T=10$ periods, divided into $K=5$ treatment cohorts (treatment periods $g \in \{3,4,5,6,7\}$) of 90 units each and a never-treated group of $N_\infty = 50$ units (10 percent of the sample). Reducing the never-treated share below the commonly used one-quarter forces TWFE to rely on already-treated cohorts as implicit controls in later calendar periods, which is the realistic scenario motivating the CAT framework. Treatment effects are cohort-heterogeneous with a monotone pattern that maximizes contamination: $\delta_3 = 1.8,\; \delta_4 = 1.5,\; \delta_5 = 1.0,\; \delta_6 = 0.5,\; \delta_7 = 0.2$, so the true equal-cohort-weight ATT is $\bar\delta = 1.0$. These fixed values are chosen so that early adopters---the cohorts most likely to serve as ``controls'' for late adopters---have the highest treatment effects, which is the configuration that inflates TWFE bias most severely (cf.\ \citealt{GoodmanBacon2021}). Parallel trends holds by construction: $Y_{it}(0) = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \varepsilon_{it}$, where $\alpha_i$ is a unit fixed effect, $\lambda_t$ is a time fixed effect, and $\varepsilon_{it} \sim N(0, 1)$.

For TWFE, we estimate $Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \beta D_{it} + \varepsilon_{it}$, where $D_{it} = \mathbf{1}\{t \geq G_i,\, G_i < \infty\}$, with confidence intervals from cluster-robust standard errors at the unit level. Because treatment effects are heterogeneous and the never-treated group is small, the TWFE coefficient receives substantial weight from already-treated cohorts used as implicit controls, leading to bias via the Goodman-Bacon decomposition.

For CSDiD-CAT and DDD-CAT, point estimates use a two-step aggregation: cohort-time DiD cells are first combined within each cohort via precision-weighted GMM (step 1), then averaged equally across cohorts (step 2), targeting $\bar\delta$. Confidence intervals are constructed via a unit-level block bootstrap with $B = 199$ draws, which correctly propagates within-cohort correlation arising from the shared pre-period baseline across multiple post-treatment DiD cells.

\subsection{Results}

Table \ref{tab:mc} reports the simulation results across 1,000 replications with $N=500$ and $T=10$.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering

\label{tab:mc}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}

\end{minipage}

Several findings emerge. TWFE exhibits a bias of approximately 12 percent of the true ATT, arising from the use of already-treated cohorts as implicit comparison groups for later cohorts. This is precisely the contamination that the CAT makes visible: early adopters with the highest treatment effects serve as ``controls'' for late adopters, dragging the TWFE coefficient below the true ATT. Coverage for TWFE falls to 42 percent, far below the nominal 95 percent, confirming that cluster-robust standard errors do not correct for this type of misspecification.

By contrast, all four CAT estimators are approximately unbiased, with bias below 1 percent of the true ATT in absolute value. Coverage ranges from 93.0 to 95.2 percent across the four variants, all close to the nominal 95 percent. The two DDD-CAT estimators---using never-treated and not-yet-treated comparison cohorts respectively---yield identical point estimates in this DGP (both $+$0.7 percent bias) and very similar coverage (93.7 and 93.0 percent), confirming that the choice of comparison group does not materially affect performance when parallel trends holds for both. RMSE is substantially lower than TWFE for all four CAT estimators, reflecting both the reduced bias and the more appropriate use of comparison units.

\begin{remark}
These results are consistent with the findings of \citet{Baker2022}, who document similar patterns of TWFE bias in corporate finance applications, and with the theoretical analysis of \citet{GoodmanBacon2021}, who characterizes the sources of that bias. The CAT provides a practical, visual tool for diagnosing and correcting the problem before estimation.
\end{remark}

\section{Application: Psychedelics Deprioritization and Crime}
\label{sec:psychedelics}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

To illustrate how the CAT framework clarifies identification in practice, I analyze the effects of psychedelics deprioritization policies on crime rates in California cities.

\subsection{Policy Context}

Between 2019 and 2023, a growing number of United States cities adopted policies to deprioritize enforcement of laws against possession or use of plant-based psychedelics. These policies typically instruct law enforcement to treat psychedelics offenses as the lowest priority, without formally legalizing the substances.

Proponents argue these reforms reduce unnecessary incarceration and align drug policy with evidence on psychedelics' therapeutic potential. Critics worry about potential spillover effects on public safety and drug markets. Despite intense policy interest, empirical evidence remains scarce.

\subsection{Data and Design}

I construct a city-month panel dataset covering 289 California cities from January 2017 to December 2023, yielding 72 months of observations. Crime data come from California DOJ monthly crime reports.

Five cities adopted psychedelics deprioritization during the study period: Oakland (June 2019), Santa Cruz (January 2020), Arcata (October 2021), San Francisco (September 2022), and Berkeley (July 2023). All other cities (284 cities) served as the never-treated comparison group.

These insights are immediately visible in the tree diagram; they are not apparent from the TWFE regression output.

\subsection{Identification Strategy}

For each cohort $g$ and time $t \geq g$, I estimate ATT$(g,t)$ using the doubly robust CSDiD estimator with never-treated and not-yet-treated cities as comparisons:
\begin{align*}
\text{ATT}(A, t) &= \mathbb{E}[Y_t(A)] - \mathbb{E}[Y_t(B, C, D, E, F)], \\
\text{ATT}(B, t) &= \mathbb{E}[Y_t(B)] - \mathbb{E}[Y_t(C, D, E, F)], \\
\text{ATT}(C, t) &= \mathbb{E}[Y_t(C)] - \mathbb{E}[Y_t(D, E, F)], \\
\text{ATT}(D, t) &= \mathbb{E}[Y_t(D)] - \mathbb{E}[Y_t(E, F)], \\
\text{ATT}(E, t) &= \mathbb{E}[Y_t(E)] - \mathbb{E}[Y_t(F)].
\end{align*}

Parallel trends requires that, absent deprioritization, each treated city would have trended similarly to its comparison group, conditional on covariates including city population, median income, and prior crime trends.

\subsection{Why the CAT Matters Here}

The CAT provides three specific contributions to this application. First, comparison group clarity: it is immediately clear which cities contribute to each cohort's counterfactual. For Oakland, the effect is identified by comparing its post-2019 crime trends to a weighted average of Santa Cruz (pre-2020), Arcata (pre-2021), San Francisco (pre-2022), Berkeley (pre-2023), and all 284 never-treated cities. Second, sample size assessment: the CAT shows that each treated cohort contains exactly one city, flagging a statistical power concern that would otherwise require careful reading of a data table. Third, assumption transparency: parallel trends requires Oakland to have trended like its comparison cities absent the policy change. The CAT helps researchers evaluate this assumption by highlighting the specific comparison cities.

\section{Application: Medicaid Expansion and Hospital Outcomes}
\label{sec:medicaid}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The second application illustrates the DDD framework using hospital-level data on Medicaid expansion under the Affordable Care Act.

\subsection{Policy Context}

The Affordable Care Act (2010) included a provision for states to expand Medicaid eligibility to all adults with incomes below 138 percent of the federal poverty level. Following the Supreme Court's 2012 decision in NFIB v. Sebelius, states gained the option to adopt or forgo expansion. By 2025, 40 states plus the District of Columbia had expanded Medicaid, while 10 states had not.

Existing evidence documents that expansion increased insurance coverage, hospital revenue, and utilization while reducing uncompensated care \citep{Guth2020, Sommers2017}. Non-profit hospitals---which account for approximately 60 percent of United States hospitals and are legally required to provide community benefit in exchange for tax-exempt status \citep{Rosenbaum2015}---tend to serve higher proportions of Medicaid patients.

Research question: Did Medicaid expansion differentially affect non-profit hospitals compared to for-profit hospitals? If expansion increases Medicaid enrollment, we expect larger effects on non-profit hospitals. This heterogeneity motivates a DDD design, differencing across expansion vs.\ non-expansion states, before vs.\ after expansion, and non-profit vs.\ for-profit hospitals.

\subsection{Data and Design}

Hospital data are drawn from \citet{GonzalezKilankoQuandt2026}, who construct a hospital-year panel covering 3,495 hospitals across all 50 United States states from 2012 to 2025. Treatment cohorts are states that expanded Medicaid in years 2014, 2015, 2016, 2019, 2020, 2021, 2023, or 2025, plus a never-expanded group. Subgroups are non-profit hospitals ($Q = 1$) and for-profit hospitals ($Q = 0$).

\subsection{Identification Strategy}

For the 2014 expansion cohort at time $t$, the DDD estimator using never-expansion states as comparison is:
\begin{align*}
\text{ATT}(2014, t) &= \left[ (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{A,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{A,t}) - (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{B,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{B,t}) \right] \\
&\quad - \left[ (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{Q,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{Q,t}) - (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{R,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{R,t}) \right],
\end{align*}
where $A$ and $B$ are the non-profit and for-profit leaves of the 2014 cohort, and $Q$ and $R$ are the non-profit and for-profit leaves of the never-expansion group. This application uses a separate A-through-R labeling (nine cohorts $\times$ two subgroups = 18 leaves) that is specific to Figure~\ref{fig:medicaid_cat} and distinct from the A-through-N scheme of Section~\ref{sec:ddd}. This is repeated for each expansion cohort.

Using not-yet-expansion cohorts as additional comparisons yields further estimates for the 2014 cohort. For example, using the 2015 expansion cohort (leaves $C$, $D$) as the comparison group:
\[
\text{ATT}_{2014,2015} = \left[ (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{A,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{A,t}) - (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{B,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{B,t}) \right]
- \left[ (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{C,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{C,t}) - (\bar{Y}^{\text{post}}_{D,t} - \bar{Y}^{\text{pre}}_{D,t}) \right].
\]
Analogous expressions hold using cohorts 2016 ($E$, $F$), 2019 ($G$, $H$), 2020 ($I$, $J$), 2021 ($K$, $L$), 2023 ($M$, $N$), and 2025 ($O$, $P$) as not-yet-expansion comparisons. All estimates for the 2014 cohort are combined via GMM as described in Section~\ref{sec:ddd}, and the same procedure applies to each subsequent expansion cohort.

\subsection{Why the CAT Matters Here}

The CAT provides three key insights for this application. First, it reveals the DDD logic visually: each expansion cohort has two subgroups (non-profit and for-profit), and the never-expansion states also have two subgroups. The DDD contrast asks whether the non-profit/for-profit gap widened more in expansion states than in non-expansion states. Second, the tree immediately flags the 2025 cohort as potentially underpowered ($n = 56$), guiding the researcher to report that cohort's estimates with appropriate caveats. Third, the tree shows the over-identification structure: the 2014 cohort has seven valid comparison cohorts, while the 2025 cohort has only one. This asymmetry is invisible in a standard TWFE regression.

\section{Software: The catviz R Package}
\label{sec:software}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The CAT framework is implemented in the catviz R package. The package is available on CRAN and can be installed once with:

\begin{verbatim}
install.packages("catviz")
\end{verbatim}

In each subsequent R session, load the package with:

\begin{verbatim}
library(catviz)
\end{verbatim}

The typical workflow involves three steps. Step 1 builds the CAT specification from panel data:

\begin{verbatim}
spec <- cat_spec(data, id = "hospital_id", time = "year",
                 g = "g", subgroup = "p", group_id = "state")
\end{verbatim}

Step 2 labels cohorts with letters in chronological order:

\begin{verbatim}
spec <- cat_label(spec)
\end{verbatim}

Step 3 generates and saves the CAT diagram. The function cat\_plot\_tree automatically detects the design type (DiD, CSDiD, or DDD) from the spec object and dispatches to the appropriate plot function:

\begin{verbatim}
cat_plot_tree(spec, counts = TRUE)
cat_plot_tree(spec, grayscale = TRUE)
\end{verbatim}

The grayscale option produces a black-and-white version suitable for journals that do not support color figures.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

This paper introduces the Causal Assignment Tree, a formal and visual framework that makes the counterfactual structure of DiD estimators transparent and verifiable. We provide a formal definition (Definition 1), establish nonparametric identification (Proposition 1), prove completeness (Proposition 2), and characterize the TWFE validity condition (Corollary 1). Monte Carlo evidence confirms that CAT-guided estimators dramatically outperform TWFE under heterogeneous treatment timing, reducing bias from 12 percent to under 1 percent and restoring confidence interval coverage to near-nominal levels (93--95 percent across all four CAT variants versus 42 percent for TWFE).

\subsection{Main Contributions}

Three contributions stand out. First, the CAT provides a unified framework spanning 2$\times$2 DiD, CSDiD, and DDD. All three designs follow the same logic: construct the counterfactual by selecting valid comparison leaves under conditional parallel trends. The CAT makes this shared logic visible in a single diagram. Second, the formal framework in Section \ref{sec:formal} restates identifying assumptions as conditions on leaf pairs, making their content transparent and their plausibility assessable. This is a methodological contribution independent of the visualization. Third, the catviz R package makes the framework immediately usable. Researchers with standard panel data can generate a CAT diagram and verify their comparison group structure before running a single regression.

\subsection{Practical Checklist}

The CAT framework suggests a three-step checklist for any DiD study:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Draw the CAT. Run cat\_spec and cat\_plot\_tree to visualize the design. Verify that the comparison leaves contain sensible comparison units.
\item Check for contamination. Confirm that no leaf serves as both treated and comparison in different estimands. If it does, consider using CSDiD or DDD instead of TWFE.
\item Assess power. Examine the sample sizes in each leaf. Flag any cohort with fewer than 20--30 units for special attention in reporting.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}

The CAT framework has two principal limitations. First, it is a visualization and diagnostic tool, not a test. The CAT clarifies which comparisons are being made, but it cannot verify whether the parallel trends assumption holds. Researchers still need to test for pre-trends, assess covariate balance, and consider sensitivity analysis using tools such as \citet{RambachanRoth2023}. Second, with many treatment cohorts, the tree becomes visually complex. Researchers with 20 or more cohorts may prefer to use the tree conceptually (grouping cohorts by adoption timing) rather than literally.

\subsection{Future Extensions}

Several extensions are natural. The CAT framework could be extended to accommodate continuous treatment intensity, time-varying treatment status where units can switch in and out of treatment, multiple simultaneous treatments, and high-dimensional covariate adjustment. Connecting the CAT to synthetic difference-in-differences \citep{Arkhangelsky2021} is also a promising direction, as both methods share the goal of making comparison group selection explicit.

\subsection{Closing Remarks}

Causal inference is fundamentally about counterfactuals: comparing what happened to what would have happened. DiD methods construct these counterfactuals using comparison groups under parallel trends. The CAT makes this process visible. By showing exactly how the counterfactual is built, the tree helps researchers think clearly about identification, communicate their designs effectively, and build credible evidence for policy.
